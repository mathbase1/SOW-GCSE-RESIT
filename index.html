Planning guide

Use these principles to adapt the plan to the learners in front of you. Keep the mathematical goal clear, use evidence to adjust the next step, and make support progressively less necessary.

Start with the intended mathematical thinking

Write an outcome that a learner can demonstrate. Move from identifying and using a method towards comparing approaches, explaining a decision or correcting a convincing error.

Bloom’s categories can help vary the thinking required. A useful extension may involve a stronger explanation or a different representation, while keeping the same accessible numbers.

Plan access through more than one route

  • Engagement: offer a relevant context or a limited choice of practice task, with a clear purpose and achievable next step.
  • Representation: connect diagrams, objects, spoken explanation and notation; explain unfamiliar vocabulary as it appears.
  • Action and expression: allow learners to demonstrate thinking through a model, an oral explanation or written work, then practise the form needed for the assessment.

Make scaffolding responsive

Use a worked example, a partially completed solution or a visual organiser when it addresses a specific obstacle. Remove a prompt once the learner can explain and use that step independently.

Retain worthwhile challenge for secure learners through unfamiliar contexts, method comparisons and justification. Avoid making extra copying the reward for early completion.

Let assessment change what happens next

Use a short diagnostic to establish a starting point. During teaching, ask a question that exposes the likely misconception, collect an individual response and decide whether to continue, revisit or regroup.

A planned formal checkpoint serves a different purpose from these formative checks. Confirm its timing and conditions locally. In tutor reflections, record the evidence, the feedback given and the resulting next action.

The Core 8 in everyday planning

These prompts translate the college’s eight principles into decisions that can be checked in a lesson plan or reviewed after teaching.

Planning lensWhat to look for
1 · Participation and confidenceEstablish respectful routines, use mistakes constructively and ensure that each learner has a practical way to contribute.
2 · Connected learningIdentify the prerequisite knowledge, the new mathematical step and the later task that will draw on it.
3 · Meaningful contextsChoose a setting that clarifies the mathematics and gives the final answer a quantity, unit or decision to interpret.
4 · Purposeful tasksMatch each activity to its intended learning. Make the expected mathematical product or explanation clear.
5 · Revealing questionsUse contrasting examples and plausible incorrect answers to uncover thinking, then ask learners to justify their choice.
6 · Ambition with supportKeep the goal worthwhile, offer precise temporary scaffolds and extend reasoning when learners are ready.
7 · Review and retrievalReturn to important ideas across topics, revisit an earlier error and check whether a method can be used without its original prompts.
8 · Feedback that leads to actionIdentify the next mathematical improvement, provide time to act on it and check the revised response.

Calibrate the Foundation route

Build secure core knowledge before increasing complexity. Use two-circle Venn diagrams and two-dimensional Pythagoras for the main route. Expanding two binomial brackets and solving pairs of simultaneous linear equations belong within the Foundation content; develop them through structured examples and clear checks. Three-dimensional distance tasks and more involved set diagrams can be optional extensions. For grouped data, distinguish an estimated mean from an exact mean and identify the median class when the individual values are unavailable.

Resource banks may mix tiers and levels. Select the questions that match the intended outcome, and use the official Foundation specification and teaching guidance when deciding the required depth.
